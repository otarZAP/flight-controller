00

Two domains, and the four wires between them

Every decision in this build comes out of one idea, so it is worth stating before anything else. There are two electrical worlds on this aircraft and they do not mix.

The quiet domain is logic: a 3.3 V microcontroller, an IMU resolving milli-g, a serial receiver. The power domain is a 4S pack shoving a hundred amps of peak phase current through six MOSFETs per motor, switching at tens of kilohertz with nanosecond edges. If those two worlds share a return path, the second one writes noise directly onto the first one's ground reference — and your gyro reads it as motion.

That is the whole thesis. Separate boards, star grounding, the soft-mounted IMU, the keep-out on the perfboard, the phase-2 PCB layout rules — every one of them is the same idea applied at a different scale.

Why perfboard before PCB

On perfboard, a noise problem is fixed by moving a wire. On a PCB it is fixed by respinning a board and waiting two weeks. Build the version you can debug first, then commit the proven layout to copper — the integrated board is phase 2, and its job is to productize something that already flies, not to solve firmware and mixed-signal layout at the same time.

01

Parts, and why these ones

The brain: Teensy 4.1

dRehmFlight is written for, documented against, and flown on the Teensy 4.0/4.1. Running it on the board it was written for is not a compromise — it is the option that removes the most work, and it removes the riskiest work. The pin map, the wiring diagrams, and every piece of community troubleshooting all target this exact part.

  • 600 MHz Cortex-M7, bare metal. No RTOS, no scheduler, no second core to reason about. A loop that takes 400 µs takes 400 µs every time, which is the whole game for a rate controller.
  • Every library already works. PulsePosition, PWMServo, the bit-banged OneShot125 output — all native. Nothing to replace.
  • Real hardware watchdog. The i.MX RT1062 carries independent WDOG units that reset the chip regardless of what the CPU is doing. See §08 — this is strictly stronger than a software task watchdog.
  • Eight hardware serial ports. Receiver, telemetry, GPS and a debug console with no bit-banging and no contention.

Teensy 4.x is NOT 5 V tolerant

This is the one that destroys boards, and it catches people precisely because the Teensy 3.x line was tolerant on its digital pins. Every I/O pin on a 4.1 is 3.3 V maximum, and there is no protection diode to save you. Before any signal wire touches a pin, meter it. An ELRS receiver's SBUS output is 3.3 V and fine; a 5 V servo tester, a BEC-powered peripheral, or a logic-level shifter wired backwards is not.

Other 4.1 pins that will bite you

Pin 13 is both SPI SCK and the onboard LED — harmless, but the LED will flicker on every SPI transfer and it is confusing during bring-up if you forget. Analog inputs are 3.3 V full-scale, so size the battery divider against 3.3 V, not 5 V. The bottom-side pads (SD card, PSRAM, Ethernet) are easy to forget exist — check them before declaring a pin free.

The ESP32-S3 stays a good board, just not for this job — it loses on the one axis that matters, which is that it requires porting work the Teensy does not. The ESP32-P4 is ruled out: Arduino support for it is still explicitly immature, with ESP-IDF being the recommended path, and a flight-critical loop is the wrong place to sit on a maturing toolchain. Keep both for the RF work.

02

The port you deleted

An earlier revision of this manual planned an ESP32-S3 and a port of dRehmFlight onto it. That plan is abandoned, and the section that used to live here was a catalogue of everything the port would cost. It is worth keeping the catalogue, because it is now a list of work that does not exist — and because it shows what "run it on the board it was written for" actually buys.

PulsePosition.h

What this reversal is actually worth

Six categories of work, every one of them in the flight-critical path, and every one of them the kind that fails intermittently rather than obviously. Trading them away for a board already sitting on the bench is not a compromise or a downgrade — it is the largest single risk reduction available to this project, and it cost nothing.

What is left to do

Very little, which is the point. Run dRehmFlight close to stock for first flight: set the IMU type, set the receiver protocol, set the mixer for a quad, and calibrate. Resist the urge to restructure it before it has flown — every line you change is a line that was flight-proven and now isn't. The VTOL mixer work comes later, on a codebase you have already seen fly.

03

Perfboard layout

The board is a plan drawing, not an accident of where the parts happened to fit. Three zones, in a fixed order, with the noisiest thing furthest from the most sensitive thing.

Rules that survive the move to PCB

  • Power in at one end, signal out at the other. Never route a motor signal wire back across the power zone to reach a header.
  • The keep-out band is real. Nothing lives in it — no resistors, no jumpers, no "just this one wire". Its whole job is to be empty.
  • Twist the pairs. Battery + and − twisted together, each motor signal twisted with its own ground return. A twisted pair encloses almost no loop area, and loop area is what couples.
  • Ceramic at the point of load. A 100 nF within a few millimetres of the Teensy's 3.3 V pin, in addition to whatever bulk sits at the buck. Bulk caps handle sag; ceramics handle edges, and they only work if they are close.
  • Strain-relieve everything. A solder joint carrying vibration is a fatigue crack with a countdown. Anchor wires mechanically before they reach the pad.

04

Grounding: the one that actually decides whether it flies

If a single section of this manual is worth re-reading before you solder, it is this one. Ground problems do not announce themselves. They present as a gyro that drifts under throttle, an aircraft that oscillates only in fast forward flight, or an autolevel that pulls to one side as the pack sags — all of which look like tuning problems and none of which are.

Practical rule

Pick one physical point — the pack negative pad on the perfboard — and mark it on the board with a Sharpie before you solder anything. Every ground wire in the build terminates there, not at whatever ground pad happens to be nearest. If you find yourself running a ground from one module to another because it is convenient, you have just built the left-hand picture.

05

The IMU soft mount is a filter, so design it like one

A soft mount is not padding. It is a mechanical second-order low-pass filter, and it has a corner frequency set by the sensor board's mass and the compliance of whatever you stick it down with. Getting it right means putting that corner well below the frequencies the airframe produces.

Run the numbers for the 250

Take a 5-inch two-blade prop on a motor spinning up to roughly 20,000 RPM under a 4S pack:

QuantityValueWhere it comes from
Motor shaft rate~333 Hz20,000 RPM ÷ 60
Blade-pass frequency~667 HzShaft rate × 2 blades — the dominant vibration line
Target mount corner30–80 HzRoughly a decade below shaft rate, so the mount is well into its attenuating region across the whole throttle range
Airframe rate content< 30 HzActual aircraft motion. The mount must pass this untouched, which is what sets the lower bound.

That window — above real flight dynamics, a decade below blade pass — is the whole design target. Too stiff and vibration reaches the sensor; too soft and the mount has its own resonance inside the flight band, which is worse than no mount at all because it amplifies rather than attenuates.

What that means in practice

  • Use gyro foam or soft double-sided tape, not rigid VHB. VHB is an adhesive, not an isolator — it is stiff enough that the corner lands right in the vibration band.
  • Mass helps. A bare breakout is very light, which pushes the corner up. Some builders deliberately add a small mass to the sensor board. If you do, keep it symmetric about the sensor.
  • Flying leads, and leave them slack. Four taut wires are four springs in parallel with your foam, and they will dominate the mount stiffness. This is the most common way a good soft mount is accidentally defeated.
  • Balance the props first. No mount fixes an out-of-balance prop, and prop imbalance is the largest single vibration source on most builds. Balance before you tune anything.

The software half

dRehmFlight carries first-order low-pass constants on the gyro and accelerometer channels. They are the right complement to a good mount, and no substitute for one: filtering costs phase lag, and phase lag in an inner rate loop is exactly what limits how hard you can tune. Fix vibration mechanically first, then filter what is left. A build that needs aggressive software filtering to fly is telling you the mount is wrong.

06

Motor output: use the hardware, not the loop

Moving to the Teensy changed this section more than any other. dRehmFlight emits OneShot125, bit-banged, and on a bare-metal M7 that is deterministic — so the output layer needs no work at all. The question is now simply whether the ESCs on the 250 accept it, and they almost certainly do.

ProtocolFrameMax rateVerdict for this build
Standard PWM1000–2000 µs~490 Hz Universally supported, and far too slow to keep up with a 2 kHz loop. Fine to prove the wiring, not to fly well.
OneShot125125–250 µs~4 kHz Use this What dRehmFlight ships, and the jitter objection that applied on an RTOS does not apply here. Zero new code.
DShot60016-bit digital~37 kHz Phase 2 Digital and checksummed, and what AM32 does best — so the custom ESCs in §10 will want it. dRehmFlight does not emit it; adding DShot on a Teensy 4.1 is a bounded job using FlexIO or DMA, but it is not first-flight work.

The ESC protocol question mostly dissolved

Practically every BLHeli_S and AM32 ESC auto-detects its input protocol on the first few frames after power-up, and OneShot125 has been in that set since the beginning. So unless the 250 is wearing something genuinely unusual, dRehmFlight's stock output will drive the ESCs already fitted with no configuration on either side.

Still confirm it rather than assume — but downgrade it in your head from "blocking decision" to "thing to verify on the bench at step 6." If the arming tone plays and the motor spins smoothly from idle, the ESC understood you.

So the output layer is finished before it is started. Keep dRehmFlight's mixer, its scaling, and its pin toggling exactly as written, and spend the attention you saved on grounding and the safety systems instead — which is where it was always better spent.

PROPS OFF

Every first run of anything in this section happens with props physically removed from the aircraft, not just throttle down. A mixer sign error at the bench costs you a startled afternoon; the same error with props on costs you a hand.

07

Radio link

The RC link is bought, not built — an external ExpressLRS module into the transmitter, and an ELRS receiver on the aircraft. That is the same reasoning as the AM32 decision in §10, applied to RF instead of power electronics: the link between your thumbs and the motors is the last place to introduce something unproven, and ELRS is years of solved problems — adaptive packet rates, frequency hopping, binding, and a failsafe detector that reliably distinguishes degraded from gone.

ELRS receivers speak CRSF natively; SBUS is a legacy output mode they can be configured into. dRehmFlight's documented receiver support is PWM, PPM, SBUS and Spektrum DSM — CRSF is not among them. That is the fork, and it is worth taking deliberately rather than by default.

Path A — ELRS in SBUS mode first flight

Configure the receiver to output SBUS. It drops straight into dRehmFlight's existing SBUS path with zero new code. You give up CRSF's return telemetry — link quality, RSSI, battery voltage back to the transmitter — which is worth having but is not worth having before the aircraft has flown.

Path B — real CRSF phase 2

Integrate CRSFforArduino, which explicitly supports Teensy 4.x, so the board choice adds no risk here. You get full bidirectional telemetry, at the cost of integrating a library dRehmFlight was not written against. Do this once you are flying and want richer link data.

Path C — homebrew LoRa dropped

ELRS is LoRa — it runs on Semtech SX127x/SX1280 silicon; that is the "LRS" in the name. Rolling your own link on a raw LoRa module means re-solving latency tuning, hopping and failsafe detection yourself, and reintroduces exactly the unverified-link risk the ELRS module was bought to remove. The spare S3 LoRa module has a good job waiting in telemetry or a failsafe position beacon — on its own channel, independent of control.

Check the inversion before you blame the code

SBUS is inverted serial by convention, but ELRS receivers can be configured to emit it either inverted or not, and the two look identical on a scope until you look at the idle level. The Teensy's LPUART can invert in hardware, so there is no transistor to add — but the setting on the receiver and the setting in the firmware have to agree. A receiver that produces perfectly valid frames the wrong way up presents as "no channels at all", which sends people hunting through their parser for hours. Meter the idle line first.

The part that is actually safety-critical

Whatever you choose, the receiver's job is not only to deliver channel values — it is to tell you, unambiguously and fast, when it has stopped delivering them. Timestamp every valid frame. If the newest good frame is older than 500 ms, that is not a stale reading to reuse; it is a failsafe condition, and it belongs in §08.

Hold-last-command is the bug

The failure mode that kills aircraft is a receiver that keeps outputting its last-known channel values after the link drops. If your parser fills a struct and the control loop reads that struct without checking its age, you have built exactly that behaviour by accident. Make the age check part of reading the data, not a separate step someone can forget.

08

Safety systems

Five mechanisms, each guarding one named failure. None of them ships unverified, and the verification is a bench test you can actually perform — not a code review.

MechanismFailure it guardsBench test
Signal-loss failsafe RX link drops mid-flight Power off the transmitter. Motors must cut. Never hold last command.
Arm / disarm state machine Spin-up on boot, on reconnect, or on a glitched throttle channel Boot with throttle high — must refuse to arm. Disarm, re-power the RX — must stay disarmed.
Hardware watchdog MCU hang Force a hang behind a debug command. The chip must reset and the motors must cut — not freeze at last throttle.
IMU sanity checks Spikes or stuck values poisoning the estimator Unplug the IMU mid-run. Reject out-of-range and unchanging samples before fusion, not after.
Brownout / VBAT monitor Sag under load resetting the MCU while ESCs stay armed Clean cutoff at threshold. Verify behaviour through a reset, not only at it.

On a Teensy this is a real watchdog, not a task watchdog

Worth being precise, because the distinction is a safety property rather than a naming detail. A task watchdog is software: a timer task notices that another task stopped checking in. It cannot help you if the thing that wedged is the scheduler, an interrupt storm, or the CPU itself — the watchdog is wedged too.

The i.MX RT1062 on the 4.1 has independent hardware WDOG units with their own clock. Once started, only a periodic feed from running code stops them resetting the chip, no matter what state the CPU is in. That is strictly stronger, and it is a benefit of the board change that is easy to overlook: what would have been a software mitigation on the ESP32 is a hardware guarantee here. Set the timeout to a few multiples of the loop period, feed it once per loop, and never from an interrupt.

Your last line of defence is not on the FC

If the Teensy hangs or browns out, the ESCs keep their own power — they do not stop because the FC stopped. What saves you is that the signal stream stops, and AM32 and BLHeli both cut a motor after their own signal-loss timeout. That timeout is genuinely the last thing standing between a hung MCU and a runaway motor, so test it deliberately: arm on the bench with props off, pull the signal wire, and time how long the motor keeps spinning. Write the number down. It is a real parameter of your aircraft.

09

Bench bring-up, in order

Each step is independently verifiable and none depends on the next one working. Do not skip ahead — the value of the order is that when something fails you know it was introduced by the step you just did.

  1. Smoke test the power path, no MCU fittedBench supply with current limit set to 200 mA, not a LiPo. Confirm 5.0 V out of the buck and correct polarity at the socket before a Teensy ever sits in it.
  2. Meter every signal line before it touches a pinThe 4.1 is not 5 V tolerant and has no protection diode. This step takes ninety seconds and is the difference between a flying aircraft and a dead board.
  3. Blink an LEDTrivial, and it proves toolchain, USB, board and power all at once. Teensyduino uploads through the Teensy Loader, so confirm the button-press flow works now rather than while debugging something else.
  4. Read the IMU, print raw valuesLevel the board and confirm roughly 1 g on Z and near zero on X and Y. Rotate it by hand and check every axis sign against the mixer convention now, on the bench, where a sign error costs nothing.
  5. Confirm loop rate under loadInstrument the loop period and log the worst case, not the average. A 2 kHz mean with occasional 8 ms outliers is a failing result that an average will hide.
  6. Bind the ELRS link and print channelsVerify every stick and switch through dRehmFlight's SBUS path. Then power the transmitter off and confirm the stale-frame detector fires within 500 ms. If you see nothing at all, suspect SBUS inversion before you suspect the parser.
  7. Drive one motor, props off, aircraft clampedOne motor. Confirm direction and that it stops when commanded. Then the other three, one at a time.
  8. Walk the arm/disarm state machineEvery transition in the diagram above, including the ones that must be refused: throttle-high arming, arming on a stale link, and auto re-arm out of failsafe.
  9. Test all five safety mechanismsThe bench tests from §08, each one performed and each result written down. This is the gate before anything spins with props fitted.
  10. Tethered hover, props on, outdoorsFirst time under power. Tether it, stand clear, and have the disarm switch under your thumb before the pack goes in.
GATE

The safety-mechanism step is a hard gate, not a checklist item. If any of the five has not been demonstrated on the bench, the tethered hover does not happen — regardless of how well everything else went that afternoon.

10

The custom ESC — build the board, borrow the firmware

One ESC per motor is the right architecture, and for a stated reason: a failure is contained to one motor's authority instead of taking out the whole power stage. That is real redundancy, and it is the kind that a 4-in-1 board cannot give you.

But there is a trap inside this part of the project, and it is worth naming before you start. The hard part of an ESC is not the board. It is the commutation firmware.

This is the KISS answer, not the lazy one

Writing your own commutation is a larger project than the flight controller — and unlike the FC, where custom firmware buys you VTOL mixer logic and failsafe behaviour you actually control, a hand-written ESC loop buys you nothing that AM32 does not already do better. Design the hardware, pick a supported MCU, flash the firmware. If you later want to write commutation as its own exercise, you will have a known-good board to test it against, which is a far better position than debugging new silicon and new firmware together.

Specific FET and gate-driver part numbers move constantly with availability, and any list written today will be partly wrong in six months. Treat the categories above as the specification and confirm actual parts against current distributor stock and the AM32 project's supported-target list when you get to schematic capture.

11

Phase 2 — committing it to copper

Once the perfboard flies and the ESCs work, the integrated board becomes worth doing — less wiring, less mass, fewer connectors to shake loose. The rules are the same ones from §03 and §04, expressed in layout terms.

Split the ground pour

Separate analog/logic ground from power ground, joined at exactly one point. This is §04's star, drawn in copper. A single unbroken pour under both domains is the daisy chain wearing a disguise.

Keep the domains physically apart

FETs, phase traces and bulk caps at one end; MCU and IMU at the other. Distance costs nothing at layout time and cannot be added afterwards.

Isolate the IMU even here

An integrated board does not repeal the mechanics of §05. Soft-mount the FC section on standoffs, or give the IMU its own compliant mount — not flat against a power PCB.

Short, direct gate traces

Gate drive snaked around the board reintroduces exactly the noise you spent phase 1 eliminating. Keep every gate loop tight and its return adjacent.

Mixed analog, digital and power layout is a genuinely different discipline from the RF board work — different failure modes, different parasitics — but the governing instinct is the one you already have: keep the noisy things and the sensitive things apart, and control where the return current goes.
