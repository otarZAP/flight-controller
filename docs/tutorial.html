Start here

Hello, and what we're doing

Welcome. Over the next few evenings you're going to build a flight controller — the small computer that keeps an aircraft from falling over — on a bare piece of perfboard, from parts, with your own hands. Then you're going to fly it.

You don't need to know anything about drones to start. You need patience, a soldering iron, and a willingness to check things instead of hoping. Everything else is in here.

Before any of that, here's exactly what you're signing up for: what you'll make, what you'll be working with, and what "right" looks like. Read this once and the whole build has a shape.

What we're making

Build, program, test and fly a custom flight controller for a 250 mm quadcopter — and understand every part of it well enough to fix it, change it, and explain it to somebody else.

What you'll be working with

Given: a workbench with a temperature-controlled soldering iron, a multimeter, and a current-limited bench supply; the parts listed in Part 1; a Teensy 4.1 microcontroller; the free dRehmFlight firmware; a computer running the Arduino IDE; a 250 mm quadcopter frame with motors and ESCs already fitted; an ExpressLRS radio link; and an open grassy area away from people for flight testing.

Working: alone, over roughly three to four evenings, indoors for the build and outdoors for the flying. If you're under 18, with an adult present for battery charging and for the first flight.

Not given: prior drone knowledge, prior flight-controller experience, or an existing aircraft to copy.

What "right" looks like

  • The aircraft hovers hands-off for ten seconds without wandering or oscillating.
  • All five safety mechanisms are demonstrated on the bench, with props off, before a propeller is ever fitted.
  • Every step is finished properly before the next one starts. This is the rule that makes the whole thing work — when something breaks, you always know which step broke it.
  • Every soldered joint is shiny and cone-shaped, not dull and blobby.
  • Every ground wire runs to one single point, never module to module.
  • You can point at any wire on the finished aircraft and say what it does and why it's there.

Staying safe, legal and decent

Rules differ by country, so check your own aviation authority before you fly — but these hold almost everywhere and none of them are optional:

  • Register the aircraft if it's over your country's weight threshold. A 250 mm quad with a 4S pack is often right around it.
  • Keep it in sight, below roughly 120 m / 400 ft, and well away from airports and helicopter routes.
  • Never over people, crowds, or roads. Not "carefully over" — not over.
  • Not over property that isn't yours, and no filming people who haven't agreed to it.
  • Charge LiPo batteries in a fireproof bag, never unattended, never overnight. This is the one that burns houses down.
  • Props off until step 13. Everything in this guide is written so you never need to break this rule.
Nugget

Writing those three things down before starting is the oldest trick in teaching anything practical, and it works because it removes the two things that quietly wreck learning: not knowing what "finished" actually means, and not knowing whether a problem is your mistake or your equipment. Pin those down first and you can teach yourself almost anything.

Every part of this guide repeats that pattern in miniature, and every single build step ends with a "you're done when" — something you can actually see or measure, not a vague "it should work now". Hit it and move on. Miss it and you stop and fix it right there, while it's still cheap.

Part 0

What you're actually building

Five minutes of reading that makes the other thirty steps make sense. Don't skip it — almost every mistake later comes from not having this picture in your head.

A flight controller is a small computer that sits in the middle of an aircraft and does one job, extremely fast: it notices the aircraft is tipping, and changes the motor speeds to un-tip it.

That's it. That's the whole idea. Everything else is detail.

A quadcopter is physically unstable — balanced on four columns of thrust, it wants to fall over the way a pencil balanced on your fingertip wants to fall over. You keep the pencil up by making tiny corrections constantly. The flight controller does exactly that, about two thousand times per second, forever.

Nugget

Without a flight controller a quadcopter is unflyable by a human. Not "hard" — genuinely impossible. The corrections needed are faster than human reaction time by a factor of about a hundred. Every multirotor that has ever flown, flew because a computer was doing this.

How a quadcopter steers

Four motors, no moving control surfaces, no tail rotor. Everything the aircraft does, it does by making some props spin faster than others.

Nugget

This is why your props come in two flavours and why fitting one backwards is such a classic disaster. Get a rotation direction wrong and the aircraft doesn't just yaw oddly — it flips itself into the ground the instant you throttle up, because the controller's corrections are now pushing the wrong way.

The loop that does all of it

Your flight controller runs the same three steps over and over. That repeating cycle is called the control loop, and how fast it runs is the single most important number in the whole project.

Going deeper

PID stands for Proportional, Integral, Derivative — three ways of reacting to the same error. P is "how far off am I right now", I is "how long have I been off", D is "how fast is it getting worse". Mix those three and you get a correction that's strong but doesn't overshoot. You'll tune these numbers in step 14, and it's the most satisfying part of the whole build.

The one idea that decides whether it flies

Here's a thing nobody tells beginners, and it's the reason most first-time builds fly badly: there are two completely different electrical worlds on this aircraft, and they must not touch.

The quiet side

Your Teensy and the IMU. Tiny voltages, delicate measurements. The IMU is detecting movements smaller than a fly landing on the frame.

The loud side

The battery and the motors. Around a hundred amps at peak, switching on and off tens of thousands of times a second. Electrically it is a thunderstorm.

If those two share a wire, the thunderstorm gets written on top of the delicate measurement, and your flight controller sees movement that isn't happening. It then "corrects" for that imaginary movement, and your aircraft wobbles for no visible reason.

Throughout this guide, teal means quiet side and orange means loud side. When you see them in a diagram, that's what it means.

Tip

If you remember exactly one sentence from this entire guide, make it this one: every ground wire goes back to one single point, and never from one module to another. That single habit prevents more flight problems than any amount of tuning. Step 5 is where you do it.

Read this before you buy anything

Lithium battery packs can start fires that water will not put out, and a spinning propeller will take skin off a hand without slowing down. This is a genuinely safe hobby if you follow two rules: props stay off the aircraft until step 13, and charge batteries in a fireproof bag, never unattended, never overnight. Nothing in this guide requires you to break either rule. If you are under 18, do the battery charging and the first flight with an adult present.

Why the Teensy — and what else would work

This guide uses a Teensy 4.1, but you may already own something else, or just want to know what makes a chip suitable. Four things matter, and the first one matters far more than people expect.

  1. A hardware floating-point unit. The maths that works out which way is up uses decimal numbers, thousands of times a second. A chip with an FPU does that in hardware; one without has to fake it in software, taking tens of times longer. This single feature rules more chips out than everything else combined.
  2. Speed with room to spare. You need 2,000 complete laps a second with headroom left over. Running at 95% capacity means the first thing you add breaks it.
  3. Predictable timing. Nothing else may interrupt the loop at an unpredictable moment. This is why a bare chip beats one running an operating system.
  4. Enough pins and timers for four motors, a sensor, a radio and some spare.

Nugget

The floating-point thing is worth feeling properly. On a chip with an FPU, multiplying two decimals is one instruction — a couple of nanoseconds. Without one, the compiler builds that same multiplication out of dozens of whole-number steps. Do that a few hundred times per lap, two thousand laps a second, and a chip that looked fast enough on paper simply runs out of time. It's the difference between a calculator and doing long multiplication on paper.

Tip — the honest alternative

You can buy a finished flight controller for around $25 and flash Betaflight onto it, and it will fly better than this build on day one. That's a completely reasonable thing to do, and if your goal is "I want a drone", do that instead.

The reason to build this one is that at the end you'll understand every wire, every setting and every failure mode — and you'll be able to change things a commercial board won't let you. You're buying knowledge, not hardware. Just be clear with yourself about which one you're shopping for.

Tools you'll need

A temperature-controlled soldering iron, a multimeter, wire strippers, small side cutters, heat-shrink, and a bench power supply with an adjustable current limit. That last one is the important one.

Tip

A current-limited bench supply is the single best investment in this hobby. Set the limit to 200 mA and a wiring mistake becomes a supply that politely refuses to deliver power, instead of a puff of smoke and a $35 board in the bin. If you don't own one, this is the moment to buy the cheapest usable one you can find.

Nugget

The Teensy 4.1 runs at 600 MHz. That is faster than the desktop PCs people used to browse the early internet on, sitting on a board smaller than a stick of gum, costing $35, and you're going to use it to keep a plastic frame from falling over. Computing got weird.

If you've never soldered before

You'll do maybe fifty joints in this build. None of them are hard, and the whole skill fits in five rules.

  1. Heat the parts, not the solder. Touch the iron to the pad and the wire together for a second or two, then feed solder into the joint. The metal should melt the solder, not the iron.
  2. A good joint is shiny and cone-shaped. A bad one is dull and blobby — that's a "cold joint", where the solder stuck without properly bonding. It'll work on the bench and fail in the air.
  3. Tin your wires first. Melt a little solder into the bare wire end before joining it to anything. It makes every subsequent step easier.
  4. Set the iron around 350 °C. Too cold makes cold joints; too hot lifts pads off the board and damages parts.
  5. Wipe the tip often on a damp sponge or brass wool. A dirty tip transfers almost no heat, which feels exactly like a broken iron.
Tip

Practise on a scrap of perfboard before touching the Teensy. Ten throwaway joints will teach you more than an hour of reading, and it means your first real joint isn't also your first joint ever.

Watch out

Solder fumes are flux burning, not lead, and you shouldn't breathe them either way. Work somewhere ventilated and don't lean directly over the joint. Wash your hands before eating — especially if you're using leaded solder, which is easier to work with and worth using for a first build.

Resist the urge to tidy the code before it flies. Refactor later, from a known-good starting point.

Part 3

Build it

Fourteen steps. Each one is a single sitting and ends with something you can check, so if it breaks you always know which step broke it. Don't skip ahead — the order is the whole point.

1

Set up so you can't hurt yourself

20 min · no soldering yet

Before any electronics: clear a bench, put the LiPo in its fire bag on the far side of the room, and put the propellers in a drawer. Actually in a drawer. Not on the shelf where you can see them.

Now set your bench supply to 5 V with a 200 mA current limit and confirm the limit works by shorting the leads together briefly — the supply should clamp and show ~200 mA rather than sparking.

Watch out

The Teensy 4.1 is a 3.3 volt board and it is not 5 volt tolerant. There is no protection diode inside. Putting 5 V on any signal pin kills it instantly and permanently. This catches experienced people, because older Teensy boards were tolerant. Meter every signal line before it touches a pin — it takes ninety seconds and saves $35 and a week of waiting.

You're done when

Props are in a drawer, LiPo is bagged, and your supply clamps at 200 mA when you short it.

2

Test the power path with nothing valuable attached

30 min · first soldering

Solder the buck converter to your perfboard at one end, with the battery connector next to it. Nothing else on the board yet.

Feed it from the bench supply — not the LiPo — at around 12 V, and turn the little adjustment screw on the buck until the output reads exactly 5.00 V on your meter. These modules ship set to random voltages, and some ship set high enough to destroy whatever you plug in.

Tip

Turn the adjustment screw slowly and watch the meter as you go. Many of these modules are multi-turn and feel like nothing is happening for the first several rotations. Nothing is happening. Keep going.

Then check polarity at the socket where the Teensy will sit. Red probe on the pin that will be 5 V, black on the pin that will be ground. If that reads negative, you have the buck backwards and you just saved your board.

Photo 01 Your buck converter soldered in, meter probes on the output showing 5.00 V. step-02-power-path.jpg
You're done when

Meter reads 5.00 V at the socket, correct polarity, and the supply is drawing only a few milliamps.

3

Make a light blink — your first win

30 min · first code

Install the Arduino IDE and then Teensyduino, which adds Teensy support. Plug the Teensy into your computer with a USB cable — not into your perfboard yet — and load the Blink example.

Uploading a Teensy is slightly unusual: the Teensy Loader window pops up and sometimes asks you to physically press the button on the board. Do that now, while nothing else can be blamed.

The onboard LED should blink. That single blink proves your computer, your USB cable, your toolchain and your board all work — four things at once, none of which you now have to wonder about later.

Nugget

There's a reason every embedded project starts with a blinking light. It's the smallest possible program that proves the entire chain from your keyboard to physical reality is intact. Old-timers call it "hello world for hardware", and skipping it is how people spend a Saturday debugging a flight controller that was never running any code at all.

Photo 02 The Teensy with its LED lit. This is the "it's alive" picture — everyone should have one. step-03-first-blink.jpg
You're done when

The onboard LED blinks once per second and the IDE reports a successful upload.

4

Plan the board before you solder it

45 min · layout + sockets

This is the step people skip, and it's the step that decides whether the aircraft flies well. You're going to place parts so the loud side and the quiet side stay far apart.

Solder the sockets for the Teensy now, and the connector headers on the right edge. Leave the middle band bare. Keep the wiring map open beside you — it tells you which header is which.

Photo 03 Your board with sockets and headers in, before any wiring. Take it from directly above so you can compare against the plan. step-04-layout.jpg
You're done when

Sockets and headers are soldered, the Teensy plugs in and out easily, and the middle band is empty.

5

Wire the ground the right way

45 min · the important one

Remember the one sentence from Part 0? This is where it happens. Take a marker and put a dot on the perfboard at your star ground point, right by the battery connector. Every single ground wire in this build ends at that dot.

Tip

Twist your battery pair together along its whole length, and twist each motor signal wire with its own ground return. A twisted pair encloses almost no loop area, and loop area is what picks up interference. It takes two extra minutes and it looks properly professional.

Photo 04 Close-up of your star ground point with all the ground wires arriving at it. This is the photo other builders will actually want to see. step-05-star-ground.jpg
You're done when

Every ground wire physically ends at the dot, and you can trace each one with a finger without passing through another module.

6

Float the IMU on foam

20 min · deceptively important

Do not solder the IMU flat to the board. Stick it to a small square of gyro foam, and connect it with four wires left deliberately slack.

Here's why, and it's genuinely interesting: spinning propellers shake the frame at a very specific frequency. On a 250-size quad with 5-inch props at full throttle, each blade passes a given point roughly 667 times a second. That shaking travels through the frame into your board, and the IMU cannot tell the difference between "the aircraft is moving" and "I am being vibrated".

The foam is a filter. It lets slow movements through — the aircraft actually tilting, which happens at a few times a second — and blocks fast ones.

Watch out

Four taut wires are four springs, and they will stiffen your mount right back up, undoing the foam entirely. This is the most common way a good soft mount gets accidentally defeated. Leave visible slack in every wire.

Nugget

The "gyro" in your IMU has no spinning wheel in it. It's a MEMS device — a microscopic silicon comb, vibrating, that gets deflected sideways when you rotate it. There's a machine smaller than a grain of salt doing that inside a $4 chip, and the same technology is why your phone knows which way up it is.

Photo 05 The IMU on its foam island with slack wires. Side-on shows the slack best. step-06-imu-island.jpg
You're done when

You can press the IMU down gently and feel it move, and every wire has visible slack.

7

Ask the IMU which way is down

45 min · first real sensor data

Wire the IMU per the pin table — four wires: 3.3 V (not 5 V), ground, SDA to pin 18, SCL to pin 19.

Then download dRehmFlight — the flight firmware this build uses — open it in the Arduino IDE, and work through the settings list. Upload, open the serial monitor, and print the raw sensor values.

Lie the board flat. You should see roughly 1 g on the Z axis (that's gravity, pulling down through the sensor) and close to zero on X and Y.

Now tilt it and watch the numbers move. Do this deliberately for every axis and write down which direction makes each number go positive. You are checking that the sensor's idea of "left" matches the frame's idea of "left".

Tip

Getting an axis sign backwards is the single most common cause of a quad that flips instantly on takeoff. It costs nothing to check now, on a bench, with props in a drawer. It costs a frame to discover later.

Going deeper

An accelerometer measures acceleration, and gravity is indistinguishable from acceleration — which is why it reads 1 g sitting still. That's not a quirk, it's Einstein's equivalence principle showing up in a $4 part. The gyro measures rotation rate instead, and the firmware blends both: the gyro is fast but drifts, the accelerometer is slow but always knows where down is. The blending algorithm is called a Madgwick filter, published by a PhD student in 2010, and it's now inside almost every drone on Earth.

Photo 06 Your serial monitor showing live IMU values, with the board flat. Screenshot is fine. step-07-imu-reading.jpg
You're done when

Z reads about 1 g flat, tilting changes the right numbers, and you've written down every axis direction.

8

Check how fast the loop actually runs

20 min · measure, don't assume

Print how long each pass through the control loop takes. You're aiming for around 2,000 per second, meaning each lap takes about 500 microseconds.

Here's the part that matters: log the worst lap, not the average. A loop that averages 2 kHz but occasionally stalls for 8 milliseconds is a failing result, and an average will cheerfully hide that from you.

Nugget

This build uses a Teensy specifically because it has no operating system. On a chip running an OS, something else can interrupt your control loop at an unpredictable moment and stretch a motor pulse. Here, nothing can. "Boring and predictable" is the highest compliment you can pay a flight controller.

You're done when

Worst-case lap time is consistently under about 600 µs across a minute of running.

9

Connect the radio

1 hour · expect to fiddle

Fit the ELRS module to your transmitter, bind the receiver, and set the receiver's output mode to SBUS rather than CRSF. Then wire it up: three wires, per the pin table — 5 V (red), ground (black), and the SBUS output to pin 7 (yellow).

Print the channel values and move every stick and switch. All of them, one at a time, writing down which channel each one moves.

Watch out

If you see nothing at all — not garbage, just nothing — suspect signal inversion before you suspect your code. SBUS is normally an upside-down signal, and ELRS receivers can be configured either way. A perfectly valid signal the wrong way up looks exactly like no signal. Check that setting before you spend an evening reading the parser.

Now the safety-critical half: turn the transmitter off and confirm the flight controller notices within about half a second. It must decide "the link is gone", not quietly keep using the last values it received.

Going deeper

ELRS is worth the money and it's worth knowing why. It's built on LoRa radio chips — the same long-range technology used for remote sensors — wrapped in years of engineering specifically for control: it hops between frequencies to dodge interference, changes its data rate automatically as range increases, and has a carefully tuned way of telling "weak signal" apart from "no signal". That last one is the hard part, and getting it wrong is how aircraft fly away.

Photo 07 Receiver wired in, with channel values on screen responding to a stick. step-09-radio-link.jpg
You're done when

Every stick and switch maps to a known channel, and switching the transmitter off triggers link-lost within 500 ms.

10

Spin one motor — props still in the drawer

45 min · first movement

Bolt the frame down or clamp it. Connect one motor's signal wire. Power up, arm, and give the smallest amount of throttle.

Check it spins the correct direction for its position. Then stop, connect the next motor, and repeat. One at a time, four times.

Props off. Actually off.

A mixer sign error with no props is a startled afternoon. The same mistake with props fitted is a trip to hospital. There is no version of this step that involves propellers.

Tip

Your ESCs almost certainly understand dRehmFlight's motor signal without any configuration — they auto-detect the format on power-up. If the motor plays its little startup beeps and then spins smoothly from idle, everything understood everything. If it beeps angrily and refuses, that's a signal problem, not a motor problem.

Photo 08 The frame clamped down, bare motors, no props anywhere in shot. Prove it. step-10-motor-test.jpg
You're done when

All four motors spin the right way, respond to throttle, and stop on command.

11

Teach it when it's allowed to be dangerous

45 min · the arming logic

"Armed" means the motors are allowed to spin. "Disarmed" means they absolutely are not, no matter what any other part of the code thinks. Getting this right is the difference between a hobby and an injury.

Test every transition, including the ones that must be refused: try arming with the throttle up, try arming with the transmitter off, and confirm it won't.

You're done when

You cannot arm with throttle up, cannot arm on a dead link, and never auto-rearm after a failsafe.

12

The safety gauntlet

1 hour · the gate before flight

Five tests. Perform each one for real and write down what happened. This is not a formality — it's the last point at which a mistake is cheap.

TestHowMust do
Radio lostSwitch the transmitter off mid-throttleMotors cut. Never hold the last throttle value.
Won't arm badlyBoot with throttle highRefuse to arm, and say so with the buzzer.
WatchdogAdd a debug command that hangs the code on purposeThe chip resets and motors stop, rather than freezing at the last throttle.

Going deeper

Your real last line of defence isn't on the flight controller at all. If the Teensy dies completely, the ESCs still have power — they don't stop just because the brain stopped. What saves you is that the signal stops arriving, and the ESCs cut their motors after their own built-in timeout. Test that too: arm on the bench, pull the signal wire, and time how long the motor keeps turning. Write the number down. It's a real property of your aircraft.

This is a gate, not a checklist

If any one of these five has not been demonstrated with your own eyes, step 13 does not happen today. It doesn't matter how well everything else went.

You're done when

All five tests performed and their results written in your build log below.

13

Props on. Tethered hover.

30 min · outdoors, adult present

Outside, on grass, away from people and pets. Tether the aircraft with fishing line to something heavy so it can rise a few inches and no further. Fit the props now — checking each one is the right rotation for its motor.

Stand back. Arm. Raise throttle slowly until it gets light on its feet, and watch which way it wants to lean.

Tip

Keep your thumb on the disarm switch the entire time. Not near it — on it. If anything looks wrong, disarm first and ask questions afterwards. Nothing is diagnosed better while it's spinning.

If it leans consistently one way, that's usually an axis sign or a motor order problem — go back to steps 7 and 10 rather than trying to tune it out.

Part 4

Flying it well

The build is done. This part is about the habits that keep it in one piece and the process that makes it fly beautifully instead of merely adequately.

The pre-flight ritual

Do this every single time, in this order, even when you're certain nothing has changed. Especially then — complacency is what breaks aircraft that have flown fine for months.

#CheckLooking for
1PropsTight, undamaged, correct rotation for each position. Run a fingernail along each edge — a nick you can feel is a prop you replace.
2Arms and screwsNothing loose. Vibration undoes screws, and a loose motor mount reads as a tuning problem you'll chase for hours.
3BatteryCharged, not puffy, strapped down firmly. A pack that shifts in flight moves the centre of gravity mid-air.
4WiresNothing near a prop arc. Nothing chafing. Give the IMU wires a gentle tug to confirm they're still slack, not snagged tight.
5Transmitter firstAlways power the transmitter before the aircraft, and power the aircraft down before the transmitter. Every time.
6Throttle at zeroConfirm before connecting the battery. Your arming logic should refuse anyway — this is the belt to that braces.
7Arm test on the groundArm, listen for all four motors idling evenly, disarm. Uneven idle means something changed.
8SurroundingsPeople, pets, cars, windows. Then decide where you'd rather it crashed, and stand so that's the direction away from you.
Tip

"Transmitter on first, off last" isn't superstition. If the aircraft is powered with no transmitter present, it can see an unbound or spoofed signal at exactly the wrong moment. Powering down the aircraft while the transmitter is still on means it never spends a second armed and deaf.

Tuning, properly

Tuning means adjusting how hard the DECIDE box from Part 0 pushes back. Three numbers per axis, and there's a right order to approach them.

P — Proportional

"How far off am I right now?" The main muscle. Too little and it's mushy and slow; too much and it shakes. Tune this first, and tune it alone.

I — Integral

"How long have I been off?" Fixes steady drift — wind, a slightly heavy side. Too much causes slow wallowing. Tune second.

D — Derivative

"How fast is it getting worse?" Damping — it anticipates and smooths. Very sensitive to vibration, which is why the foam mount matters. Tune last, gently.

The method

  1. Change one number, on one axis, at a time. Change two and you learn nothing from the result.
  2. Move in steps of about 20%. Small enough to be safe, big enough to feel.
  3. Fly the same test between changes — a hover, a sharp stick input, a stop. Same test every time or you're comparing nothing.
  4. Write down every value you try and how it felt, in the build log. Memory is unreliable and you will absolutely go in circles otherwise.
  5. Back off 10–15% from the point where it starts to shake. A tune that's perfect on a calm evening is marginal in wind or with a heavier battery. Leave margin.

Watch out

If you find yourself needing extreme values to make it fly, stop tuning. Something mechanical is wrong — an unbalanced prop, a loose arm, a stiff IMU mount, a bent motor shaft. Tuning cannot fix a physical problem; it can only hide it until the day it stops hiding.

Nugget

The reason too much D makes motors hot is genuinely interesting: D reacts to rate of change, so it amplifies the highest-frequency thing it can see. On a vibrating aircraft that's the vibration itself, and the controller ends up frantically twitching the motors thousands of times a second chasing shakes that aren't real movement. All that energy comes out as heat. Hot motors after a gentle flight are the controller telling you it's fighting ghosts.

Part 5 · advanced

Building your own ESCs

Optional, and genuinely harder than everything above. Do this only after the quad has flown for a while — but it's where the real power-electronics learning is.

What an ESC actually does

An Electronic Speed Controller sits between your battery and one motor. Your flight controller sends it a number meaning "spin this fast", and the ESC does the surprisingly hard work of making that happen.

Brushless motors have no brushes — no sliding contacts to switch the coils. So something else has to switch them, in the right order, at exactly the right moment, thousands of times a second. That something is the ESC.

Nugget

Here's the clever part: the ESC has no sensor telling it where the rotor is. It works it out by listening to the coil it isn't currently driving. A spinning magnet induces a small voltage in that idle coil — "back-EMF" — and the moment that voltage crosses zero tells the ESC exactly where the rotor is. It's steering by the echo of its own motion, and it does this about ten thousand times a second.

Why one per motor

Most quads use a "4-in-1" board that runs all four motors. Building four separate ESCs instead means a failure takes out one motor's control rather than the whole power stage — and it means each board is small, simple, and independently testable. That's real redundancy, and it's the same "isolate the parts" thinking as the quiet side and loud side.

The trap, and the way around it

Here's the honest bit. The hard part of an ESC is not the circuit board. It's the firmware — the code that figures out rotor position from back-EMF, gets the timing right at every speed, starts a stopped motor smoothly, and recovers when it loses track. That's a bigger project than the entire flight controller you just built.

Design targets

ThingTargetWhy
Battery voltage4S, 16.8 V maxChoose MOSFETs rated at least 30 V — switching creates voltage spikes above the battery voltage.
Current20–30 A eachPeaks go well above this. Size the copper and the cooling for the peak, not the average.

Watch out — the hidden dependency

AM32 wants DShot, and dRehmFlight doesn't send DShot — it sends OneShot125. So building your own ESCs quietly adds a firmware job on the flight controller too. It's a bounded piece of work, but plan it with the ESC boards rather than discovering it the week they arrive.

Tip

Build one ESC first, not four. Test it on a motor bolted to a heavy bench with no prop. Only when one works completely do you build the other three — otherwise you've made four copies of the same mistake.

Part 6 · roadmap, not yet flown

Where this goes next: VTOL

Everything above has been written to be built from. This part has not been built or flown — it's the plan, included so you can see where the project heads. Read it, don't solder from it.

Do not build from this section yet

The steps in Part 3 are ordered, tested and verifiable. What follows is design thinking, not verified procedure. A transitioning aircraft fails in far more interesting ways than a quadcopter does, and it fails at altitude and speed. Fly the quad for a long time first.

What a VTOL is, and why bother

A VTOL — Vertical Take-Off and Landing — lifts off straight up like a quadcopter, then tilts over and flies forward like an aeroplane, letting a wing hold it up instead of its motors.

The reason to want one is efficiency. A quadcopter in forward flight is a brick being shoved through the air by brute force, burning battery purely to stay airborne. A wing does the same job using a fraction of the power, because it converts forward motion into lift almost for free.

Nugget

A typical quad gets maybe 10–20 minutes of flight. A comparable winged aircraft carrying the same battery can stay up for an hour or more and cover many times the distance. That gap is the entire reason VTOL designs exist, and it's why they're used for surveying, search, and delivery — anywhere you need to take off in a small space but then actually go somewhere.

Three ways to build one

They differ in what physically moves, and that choice drives everything else — complexity, weight, and how badly it goes wrong when something fails.

The transition is the whole problem

Hovering is solved — you solved it in Part 3. Winged flight has been solved since 1903. The dangerous part is the ten seconds in between, when the aircraft is too fast to hover comfortably and too slow for the wing to carry it.

Going deeper

Notice that the failsafe question genuinely reverses. Everything in Part 3 said "cut the motors, never hold last command" — correct for a quad, where a dead-stick aircraft simply drops a few feet onto grass. A winged aircraft at cruise altitude with its motors cut is a glider, and a glider with sensible control surfaces can be brought down far more safely than one that's had its brain switched off. The right answer changes with the airframe, which is exactly why this needs designing rather than copying.

Why the quad had to come first

Every hour the quad flies is an hour of proof that your soldering, your grounding, your sensor mount, your radio link and your arming logic all work. When the airframe finally changes, that's the only thing that's new — and debugging one new thing is a job, while debugging six at once is a hobby you quit.

Tip

When you get here, build the wing as a plain radio-controlled aeroplane first — no lift motors, no transition, just a wing you can hand-launch and fly. Learn how it behaves. Then add the hover system to an airframe you already trust. Same principle as everything else in this guide: one new thing at a time.

Reference

Glossary

Every bit of jargon in this guide, in plain words. Skim it now, come back when something reads like nonsense.

WordWhat it means
Arm / disarmWhether the motors are allowed to spin at all. Disarmed means they cannot, no matter what any other part of the code wants.
Back-EMFThe small voltage a spinning motor generates in its own coils. ESCs listen to it to work out where the rotor is without needing a sensor.
BECA small voltage regulator built into some ESCs, giving 5 V for the flight controller. This build deliberately uses its own instead.
Brushless motorA motor with no sliding electrical contacts. More efficient and longer-lived than brushed, but it needs an ESC to switch its coils.
Control loopThe repeating sense→compare→decide→act cycle. Runs about 2,000 times a second.
CRSFThe native language ExpressLRS receivers speak. Richer than SBUS — it carries information both ways — but this build uses SBUS first for simplicity.
ESCElectronic Speed Controller. Converts "spin this fast" into the right switching pattern for a brushless motor.
FailsafeWhat the aircraft does when something goes wrong — usually cutting the motors. The most important code you'll never want to see run.
GyroMeasures how fast something is rotating. In your IMU it's a vibrating microscopic silicon structure, not a spinning wheel.
IMUInertial Measurement Unit. The chip containing the gyro and accelerometer — the aircraft's sense of balance.
LiPoLithium Polymer battery. Enormous power for its weight, and genuinely dangerous if damaged, overcharged, or shorted.
MixerThe bit of code that turns "roll left" into four specific motor speeds.
OneShot125The format your flight controller uses to tell the ESCs how fast to spin. Faster than the old servo-style signal it replaced.
PerfboardA blank circuit board covered in a grid of holes, for building one-off circuits by hand.
PIDProportional, Integral, Derivative — the three-part maths that decides how hard to push back against an error.
Pitot tubeA small forward-facing tube that measures airspeed from air pressure. Needed for VTOL, not for a quad.
Pull-up resistorA resistor that gently holds a signal wire high when nothing else is driving it. I²C connections need them.
SBUSA way of sending all radio channels down one wire as digital data. Normally an upside-down signal, which is why inversion matters.
4SA battery with four cells in series — about 14.8 V normally, 16.8 V fully charged.
Star groundWiring every ground connection back to one single physical point instead of chaining them. The most important habit in this build.
WatchdogA hardware timer that resets the chip if the code stops checking in. Your protection against a frozen flight controller.
VTOLVertical Take-Off and Landing. Takes off like a helicopter, flies like an aeroplane.
